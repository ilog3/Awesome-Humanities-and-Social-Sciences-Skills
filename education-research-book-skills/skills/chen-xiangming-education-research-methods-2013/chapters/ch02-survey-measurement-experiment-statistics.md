# Survey, Measurement, Experiment, and Statistics

## Survey

Define population, unit, sampling frame, mode, timing, constructs, and estimand. Build questions with one idea, neutral wording, suitable recall period, exhaustive and non-overlapping options, appropriate sensitivity handling, and accessible language. Pilot cognition and administration. Track coverage, sampling, nonresponse, measurement, and processing error; weight only with a defensible rationale.

## Educational measurement

Start with a construct definition and content blueprint. Specify intended use and consequences. Develop tasks/items, administration, scoring, and interpretation together. Gather evidence for content, response processes, internal structure, relations to other variables, reliability/precision, fairness, and consequences. Item difficulty or discrimination is not sufficient proof of validity. Avoid using a tool outside its validated population or purpose.

## Experiment

State treatment, comparator, unit of assignment, outcome, timing, and causal estimand. Prefer random assignment when ethical and feasible; otherwise justify quasi-experimental assumptions. Protect allocation, baseline equivalence, treatment fidelity, attrition analysis, contamination control, and consistent measurement. Consider history, maturation, testing, instrumentation, regression, selection, attrition, spillover, expectancy, and implementation threats. Report effect size and uncertainty, not p-values alone.

## Statistics

Identify variable type and distribution before analysis. Use descriptive statistics and graphics to inspect center, spread, shape, missingness, and outliers. For inference, match test/model to design and assumptions; report sample, estimate, uncertainty interval, effect size, exact analysis, assumption checks, multiplicity decisions, and sensitivity analyses. Statistical association does not establish causal or educational importance.

## Minimum quantitative audit

- construct and operational definition;
- population/sample/frame and missingness;
- instrument evidence;
- design and assignment/exposure;
- prespecified primary outcome and analysis;
- assumptions and robustness;
- practical magnitude, heterogeneity, harms, and limits.
