# Analysis, Writing, and Adequacy

## Core idea

Transform voluminous field material into a descriptive cultural account that allows readers to understand likely conduct in the setting without pretending the case is statistically general.

## Analysis procedure

1. Build a chronological inventory of days, events, and annual phases.
2. Code actors, settings, formal/informal contacts, issues, actions, judgments, and consequences.
3. Write case summaries for recurring issues.
4. Compare observed conduct with self-report, documents, and others' expectations.
5. Identify recurrent role patterns and exceptions.
6. Link patterns to system maintenance and socialization.
7. Return to fieldnotes to restore context and contrary evidence.
8. Separate description, analysis, interpretation, and evaluation in the final text.

## Worked example

If the principal appears highly visible, do not conclude “effective visibility.” Show where visibility occurs, who receives attention, which information it yields, what decisions follow, and where the principal is absent. Compare staff and student interpretations. Only then discuss whether visibility maintains access, surveillance, trust, or inequity.

## Adequacy test

A strong ethnography gives readers enough patterned detail to anticipate plausible action in comparable situations and understand why, while clearly stating what remains contingent on person, school, district, and era.

## Anti-patterns

- A day-in-the-life narrative with no analytic pattern.
- Evaluation language smuggled into description.
- Claiming transfer without contextual comparison.
- Removing ordinary detail until only abstract leadership themes remain.

## Key takeaways

1. Preserve the ordinary world that gives categories meaning.
2. Use complementary data to challenge observation.
3. Let transfer depend on explicit contextual resemblance.
4. Distinguish role explanation from leadership prescription.
