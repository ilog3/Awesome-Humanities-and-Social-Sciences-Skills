# Patterns

## Three-dimensional episode map

**When to use**: Any story, observed episode, or field text risks being read in isolation.

**How**:

1. Map past, present, and anticipated future.
2. Map inward feelings and commitments.
3. Map outward relations, institutions, and culture.
4. Name the place and how it organizes action.
5. Repeat from participant and researcher positions.

**Trade-off**: Richer context may make the narrative harder to compress; retain only relations that change interpretation.

## Research-puzzle memo

**When to use**: At entry and whenever the inquiry seems to become a conventional problem-solution project.

**How**: Write what puzzles you, the experience that produced it, why it matters personally/practically/socially, what you cannot yet know, and how the puzzle has changed.

**Trade-off**: Openness supports discovery but can cause drift; pair each revision with a recorded rationale.

## Interwoven field-text braid

**When to use**: One interview or record is becoming authoritative.

**How**: Select an episode; link conversation, observation, journal, artifact, participant-created text, and later retelling; compare how audience and time alter meaning.

**Trade-off**: More strands increase provenance and consent work.

## Relational negotiation checkpoint

**When to use**: Entry, changing focus, intimate disclosure, transition out, drafting, and publication.

**How**: Revisit relationship, purpose, transition, usefulness/validity, ownership, naming, and future reuse. Record agreement and unresolved difference.

**Trade-off**: Negotiation does not eliminate institutional power or give any party complete control over interpretation.

## Narrative-thread testing

**When to use**: Moving from field texts to research texts.

**How**: Name a candidate thread; trace it backward/forward, inward/outward, and across places; seek silences and counterstories; compare participant and researcher readings; retain, revise, or reject it.

**Trade-off**: A compelling thread can become an imposed plot; keep contradictory material visible.

## Voice-signature-reader triangle

**When to use**: Choosing research-text form.

**How**: For every section identify who speaks, whose selection is visible, and who is imagined as reader. Revise when participant quotation masks researcher authority or audience convention silences experience.

**Trade-off**: Multiplicity can impair readability; clarify positions without forcing one voice.
