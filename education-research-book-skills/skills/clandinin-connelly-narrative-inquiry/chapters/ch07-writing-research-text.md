# Chapter 9: 撰写研究文本

## Core idea

Research-text composition requires negotiating tensions among experiential complexity, scholarly conventions, participant relationships, authorial identity, and readers.

## Voice, signature, reader

- **Voice:** who can speak, in what register, with what silences and multiplicity.
- **Signature:** how the author's identity and style are present, including responsibility for selection and interpretation.
- **Reader:** the imagined audience whose expectations shape explanation, form, and accessibility.

These are relational choices. Quoting participants does not automatically give them voice, and removing first-person pronouns does not remove authorial power.

## Finding narrative form

Read other narrative works, search for an organizing metaphor, notice personal reading preferences, and try multiple forms. Possible forms include chronicle, letters, layered stories, dialogue, interludes, visual-textual composition, or conventional chapters containing sustained narratives.

Choose form by asking whether it can carry temporality, sociality, place, multiple voices, and the research puzzle. Novelty alone is not a reason.

## Worked example

A dissertation may retain conventional introduction, literature, and methodology chapters, then use letters addressed to participants for narrative chapters, followed by a cross-story interpretive discussion. The letter form works only if it reflects the actual relationship and the addressees can ethically be positioned as readers.

## Review test

Can readers distinguish field text from reconstructed scene, participant words from researcher narration, event from memory, and evidentiary claim from literary device? Can participants recognize the relational truth without being forced to approve every interpretation?

## Key takeaways

1. Write in uncertainty without making evidence ambiguous.
2. Keep authorial selection and participant positioning visible.
3. Revise form until it serves the puzzle, ethics, and reader together.
