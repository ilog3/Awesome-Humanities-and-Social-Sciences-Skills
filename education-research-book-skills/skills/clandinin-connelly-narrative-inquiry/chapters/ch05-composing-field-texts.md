# Chapter 7: 撰写现场文本

## Core idea

Field texts are interpretive compositions created within research relationships. Their variety helps preserve different dimensions of experience and prevents one transcript from becoming the authoritative record.

## Forms of field text

- teacher and participant stories;
- researcher autobiography;
- research journals and reflective notes;
- fieldnotes and reconstructed scenes;
- letters between researcher and participants;
- conversations and research interviews;
- family stories and genealogical narratives;
- documents and institutional records;
- photographs, memory boxes, and personal/social artifacts;
- remembered and presently lived experience.

## Composition rules

For every field text record author, audience, time, place, purpose, relationship, prompting conditions, revisions, consent, and later interpretation. Locate it in the three-dimensional space. Keep contradictions between texts rather than merging them prematurely.

## Interwoven field texts

Compose a braid rather than a pile. Link an interview passage to an observed episode, a letter, a photograph, the researcher's memory, and a later retelling. Each strand adds perspective and also changes the meaning of the others.

## Worked example

For a teacher's memory of a humiliating inspection, combine: her present telling; the old inspection form; a classroom photograph; a colleague's different memory; the researcher's immediate journal; and a later letter in which the teacher revises the story. Analyze how the story changes across audiences and time.

## Anti-patterns

- Calling field texts raw or unmediated.
- Treating interview transcription as the complete experience.
- Collecting intimate artifacts without renegotiating ownership and future use.

## Key takeaways

1. Diversify forms to expand experiential access.
2. Preserve provenance and relational conditions.
3. Field-text writing already begins interpretation.
