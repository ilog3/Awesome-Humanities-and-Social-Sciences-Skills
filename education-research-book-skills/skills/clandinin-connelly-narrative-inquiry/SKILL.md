## Core frameworks and mental models

### Experience is narrative

Following Dewey, treat experience as continuous and interactive. Present experience grows from earlier experience and shapes possible futures; it is simultaneously personal and social. Narrative is therefore both the phenomenon being studied and a method of inquiry.

### Three-dimensional narrative inquiry space

Locate every episode along three dimensions:

1. **Temporality** — past, present, and future.
2. **Sociality** — inward personal conditions and outward social relations.
3. **Place** — the specific physical and institutional location.

Inquiry moves backward/forward, inward/outward, and attends to place. Use these movements to prevent a story from becoming a contextless sequence or personality explanation.

### Living, telling, retelling, reliving

People live stories, tell them, retell them under new relationships, and may relive life differently through inquiry. Researcher and participant enter this cycle together. The research relation is part of the experience, not a neutral pipe carrying pre-existing stories.

### Field → field texts → research texts

Do not call field texts raw data. Notes, conversations, letters, photographs, autobiographies, and memory artifacts are already composed through relationship and interpretation. Moving to a research text requires repeated composition, justification, negotiation, and attention to audience.

### Narrative boundaries

Stay awake at two boundaries:

- **Reductionism** breaks experience into categories and loses temporal-personal unity.
- **Formalism** starts with abstract theory, certainty, and researcher distance, forcing life into a prior frame.

Narrative inquiry still uses concepts and theory, but balances them with people, uncertainty, practical context, and the unfolding research puzzle.

## Working procedure

1. Begin with a **research puzzle** rooted in lived experience, not a decontextualized problem statement.
2. Write the researcher's autobiographical narrative to expose why this puzzle calls.
3. Define the inquiry in the three-dimensional space.
4. Negotiate relationships, purposes, transitions, responsibilities, and claims to validity throughout fieldwork.
5. Compose multiple, interwoven field texts with participants.
6. Shift repeatedly between involvement and analytic distance; locate texts temporally, socially, and spatially.
7. Build narrative threads and a provisional narrative unity without erasing contradictions.
8. Decide voice, signature, form, audience, ownership, anonymity, and fact/fiction boundaries collaboratively.
9. Keep wakefulness: recheck how the emerging text may retell participants, researchers, and institutions.

## Decision rules

- Prefer a research puzzle when the inquiry seeks meaning and movement in experience; do not promise a final solution.
- A transcript is one field text, not the person's story in full.
- Anonymity can protect identity yet damage relational ownership or make recognition impossible; negotiate rather than default mechanically.
- Member response is ethically and interpretively important but does not make one version final.
- Literary form may clarify experiential meaning; it does not license invented evidence or concealed fabrication.
- Theory should enter as a conversational partner and lens, not a template that determines the plot in advance.
- Narrative validity depends on relational, temporal, contextual, and evidentiary accountability—not simply factual correspondence or emotional resonance.
