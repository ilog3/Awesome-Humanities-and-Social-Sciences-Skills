# Research Process

## Problem and literature

State a practical concern, then identify the research problem and what evidence is missing or inconsistent. Justify with literature, consequences, and audiences. A literature review locates the problem, relates it to prior findings, supplies frameworks and methods, and later supports interpretation. Record search terms, databases, criteria, quality, and synthesis.

## Purpose and questions

- Quantitative purpose: participants/population, independent/predictor and dependent/outcome variables, relations/comparisons, and setting.
- Qualitative purpose: central phenomenon, participants/site, design, and open exploratory language.
- Mixed purpose: component aims plus rationale and method of integration.

Questions must match. Hypotheses predict quantitative relations or differences; they do not belong automatically in qualitative inquiry.

## Collection

Quantitative: define population, sample, probability/nonprobability implications, instrument evidence, variables, administration, response/missingness, and data security. Qualitative: purposively select site/participants, gain permissions, negotiate researcher role, collect interviews/observations/documents/audiovisual material, use protocols, and write reflexive fieldnotes.

## Interpretation

Interpret results relative to questions, theory/literature, design strength, alternatives, limitations, and implications. Recommendations add values and feasibility assumptions; state them.

## Producer–consumer distinction

For producers, specify what to do and document. For consumers, reconstruct what was done, assess evidence, and decide whether conclusions or applications are warranted.
