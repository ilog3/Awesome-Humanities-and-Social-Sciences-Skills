# Principles, Methods, and Hidden Harm

## Three-level influence analysis

Do not isolate a parenting act from its conditions.

- **Macro:** economy, law and policy, dominant ideas of childhood, gender norms, demographic change, technology, and media.
- **Meso:** school expectations, neighborhood and community, caregiver employment, health and social services, peer networks, and family–school communication.
- **Micro:** household relationships, material resources, routines, caregiving roles, adult beliefs, child characteristics, and the immediate interaction.

Classify each influence as a resource, constraint, contradiction, or changeable condition. Then assign responsibility to the actor with the power to change it.

## Practical principles

Use the book's paired principles as tensions to manage, not rigid rules:

1. **Example and explanation:** adult conduct gives words credibility; explanation helps a child understand and judge rather than merely imitate.
2. **Expectation and respect:** communicate confidence and boundaries while preserving dignity, voice, privacy, and realistic developmental demands.
3. **Consistency and diversity:** keep core safety and ethical expectations coherent across caregivers, while adapting methods to the child and situation.
4. **Timeliness and continuity:** use teachable moments without turning every incident into a lecture; sustain routines long enough to observe change.
5. **Positive guidance and proportionate correction:** teach the desired alternative, repair harm, and restore participation. Never use humiliation, fear, violence, deprivation, or rejection as educational tools.

## Method selection

Choose a method only after specifying the intended capability or relationship change. Possible methods include modeling, dialogue, joint activity, environmental design, stories and examples, practice with feedback, agreed routines, encouragement, natural or logical consequences, and restorative repair.

For every method, ask:

- What will the child learn, beyond immediate compliance?
- Is the demand developmentally and contextually reasonable?
- Does the method protect bodily and psychological safety?
- Can the child question, explain, or propose an alternative?
- Could the same aim be reached with less coercion?
- How will adults know whether the method helped?

## Hidden-harm audit

Educational intent does not neutralize harmful effects. Audit practices for:

- public comparison, ridicule, threats, withdrawal of affection, or chronic criticism;
- contradictory rules or unpredictable punishment;
- excessive surveillance and control;
- substitution of grades, obedience, or prestige for broad development;
- adult conflict conducted through the child;
- demands that ignore disability, trauma, language, poverty, or unequal access;
- digital exposure, privacy violations, and unsafe online supervision.

If safety is at risk, prioritize protection and qualified help over method optimization.

## Worked example

**Issue:** A student repeatedly avoids homework and arguments escalate nightly.

**Weak reading:** The child lacks discipline; parents need stricter control.

**System reading:** Check task difficulty, fatigue, learning needs, school workload, device use, caregiver work schedules, household conflict, and the meaning homework has acquired in the relationship. Agree with the school on a manageable task, redesign the routine with the child's input, provide brief support and feedback, protect rest, and review evidence after a defined interval. The unit of change is the ecology and interaction—not just the child's obedience.
