# Qualitative, Historical, and Ethnographic Designs

## Qualitative design

Specify phenomenon, setting, participants, researcher role, entry, purposeful sampling, data generation, concurrent analysis, coding, deep description, credibility, and representation. The design can evolve, but decisions and consequences require documentation. Technology assists storage, retrieval, and display; it does not supply interpretation.

Assess credibility through prolonged and varied engagement, rich evidence, negative cases, comparison, participant perspectives, reflexivity, audit trail, and explicit scope. Avoid applying quantitative reliability and validity mechanically while still demanding rigor.

## Historical research

Define a bounded historical problem; seek primary and relevant secondary sources; conduct external criticism of authenticity/provenance and internal criticism of meaning, competence, purpose, bias, and context; compare independent evidence; synthesize chronology; analyze continuity, change, agency, structure, and rivals; and report uncertainty. An archive is a selected survival, not the past itself.

## Ethnographic research

Use for cultural meaning, patterned interaction, and life in natural settings. Establish a conceptual map without closing inquiry; negotiate long-term presence and roles; observe, participate, interview, and examine artifacts; generate and revise hypotheses; analyze domains, contrasts, events, language, and power; and produce a holistic but bounded account.

Do not essentialize a group as one culture, treat informants as transparent representatives, hide research reactivity, or claim complete immersion. Protect confidentiality in small communities and address colonial histories of representation.

## Shared inference rule

Distinguish description, actor meaning, researcher interpretation, historical/ethnographic explanation, evaluation, and transfer. Show evidence and disconfirming material for each major claim.
