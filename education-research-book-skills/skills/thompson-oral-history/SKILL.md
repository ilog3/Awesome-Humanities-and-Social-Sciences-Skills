# Thompson Oral History

Use Paul Thompson's oral-history framework to turn remembered experience into ethically collected, critically tested, publicly usable historical evidence.

## Core stance

- Begin with purpose: ask whose past is being reconstructed, by whose authority, and for which community or audience.
- Treat oral testimony as both evidence about events and evidence about meaning, identity, silence, and later reinterpretation.
- Do not rank written records as inherently superior. Criticize every source according to how it was produced, preserved, selected, and used.
- Preserve the narrator's life context while allowing thematic comparison across interviews.
- Keep collection, evaluation, interpretation, archiving, and presentation connected from the start.

## Workflow

1. **Frame the historical intervention.** State what established account, archival silence, social group, or community need the project addresses. Read [chapters/ch01-history-community-and-oral-evidence.md](chapters/ch01-history-community-and-oral-evidence.md).
2. **Define the evidence claim.** Separate event reconstruction, lived experience, retrospective meaning, collective memory, and narrative form. Read [chapters/ch02-achievements-and-evidence.md](chapters/ch02-achievements-and-evidence.md).
3. **Design the project.** Bound the topic, period, place, population, outputs, resources, and participation model; prepare a topic guide and sampling frame. Read [chapters/ch04-project-design-and-sampling.md](chapters/ch04-project-design-and-sampling.md).
4. **Select narrators strategically.** Match random, quota, stratified-community, network, key-informant, or theoretical sampling to the intended inference. Never claim statistical representativeness from convenience recruitment.
5. **Establish ethical terms.** Explain recording, confidentiality, attribution, withdrawal, copyright, preservation, access, and future reuse before recording. Reconfirm permissions when outputs change.
6. **Conduct an open but prepared interview.** Invite a broad life narrative, listen without needless interruption, then probe chronology, concrete incidents, meanings, contradictions, and sources. Read [chapters/ch05-interview-practice.md](chapters/ch05-interview-practice.md).
7. **Create the evidence package.** Retain the preservation master, working copy, metadata, field memo, consent and access record, transcript or indexed summary, and links to related sources. Read [chapters/ch06-preservation-transcription-and-rights.md](chapters/ch06-preservation-transcription-and-rights.md).
8. **Criticize and triangulate.** Test internal consistency, chronology, plausibility, narrator position, interviewer influence, survival bias, and agreement or productive disagreement with other sources.
9. **Interpret without flattening.** Move between whole lives and cross-case themes; preserve contrary cases, silence, emotion, language, and narrative organization. Read [chapters/ch07-interpretation-and-presentation.md](chapters/ch07-interpretation-and-presentation.md).
10. **Report calibrated conclusions.** Distinguish what the project establishes, suggests, cannot resolve, and newly enables people to ask.

## Decision rules

### Choose the interview form

- Use a **life-history interview** when sequence, turning points, identity, and relations across domains matter.
- Use a **topic-focused interview** when the claim concerns a bounded event, workplace, institution, practice, or movement.
- Use a **group interview** when interaction can recover shared vocabulary and mutually stimulated memories; document conformity and dominance risks.
- Use **repeat interviews** for complex lives, sensitive subjects, unresolved chronology, or collaborative interpretation.

### Interpret memory

- A remembered concrete practice may be more stable than a past attitude stated retrospectively.
- Telescoping, compression, omission, hindsight, public narratives, and present identity can reorganize recall.
- Inaccuracy about a date does not erase testimony's value for studying meaning; symbolic truth does not establish event fact.
- Silence is a prompt for contextual inquiry, not automatic proof of repression.
- Do not act as a therapist. Pause when distress exceeds the agreed research relationship and provide an appropriate referral route when available.

### Judge adequacy

Ask four separate questions:

1. **Authenticity:** Is the recording and record what it claims to be?
2. **Accuracy:** Which factual elements survive internal and external checks?
3. **Representativeness:** What population, experience, network, or cultural position can these narrators illuminate?
4. **Meaning:** What does the way a story is told reveal that an event ledger cannot?

## Required outputs

For a project plan, provide the historical purpose, evidence claims, narrator population, sampling logic, topic guide, ethics and rights plan, preservation workflow, source-criticism matrix, analysis strategy, limitations, and return-to-community plan.

For an evidence analysis, use the templates in [patterns.md](patterns.md) and vocabulary in [glossary.md](glossary.md). For rapid field use, consult [cheatsheet.md](cheatsheet.md).

## Guardrails

- Do not present a narrator as a passive data container; the interview is shaped by both parties.
- Do not erase grammar, dialect, hesitation, or emotion without disclosing editorial choices.
- Do not publish sensitive material merely because it was recorded.
- Do not infer prevalence from vivid testimony or treat one exemplary life as an average life.
- Do not isolate quotations from life sequence, question context, or the recording.
- Do not let obsolete recording technology in the source dictate current technical practice; retain its principles with contemporary formats.
