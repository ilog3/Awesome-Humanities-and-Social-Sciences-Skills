# Interview Practice

## Before recording

Research the person and context, but do not demonstrate expertise at the cost of listening. Prepare a flexible topic guide, chronology prompts, names and places, memory aids, and a recording check. Explain purpose, uses, access, rights, and attribution choices.

## Interview sequence

1. Establish ease and confirm consent.
2. Begin with a broad invitation about a life period, place, household, or work entry.
3. Let the narrator develop their own sequence and vocabulary.
4. Mark topics for later rather than interrupting a productive account.
5. Probe a specific incident: “Can you take me through one time that happened?”
6. Clarify chronology, roles, locations, firsthand versus heard accounts, and change.
7. Explore meanings and disagreements without prosecutorial cross-examination.
8. Ask what was missed, who else to consult, and which records or objects exist.
9. End lightly, review permissions, and explain follow-up.
10. Record a field memo immediately afterward.

## Question discipline

Ask one clear question at a time. Use open invitations before closed checks. Replace abstractions with episodes, routines, contrasts, turning points, sensory detail, and relationships. Avoid leading alternatives, moral tests, multiple questions, and vocabulary the narrator has not used.

## Listening and setting

Follow contradictions, sudden generalization, unusual precision, emotion, laughter, silence, pronoun changes, and conventional stories as cues for inquiry, not automatic suspicion. Choose a quiet, comfortable setting where privacy is controlled by the narrator.

In groups, record speaker identity, hierarchy, mutual correction, memory stimulation, joking, conformity, and suppressed disagreement. Consensus is interactional evidence, not independent corroboration.

Do not rush away when recording stops. Mark post-interview comments in the field memo and ask whether they may be added to the record. Back up files and document restrictions promptly.
