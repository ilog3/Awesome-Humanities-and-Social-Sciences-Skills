# Interpretation and Presentation

## Three broad forms

1. **Whole-life or extended narrative:** preserves sequence and interacting life domains; requires context.
2. **Collection of stories:** juxtaposes substantial accounts around a community or experience.
3. **Cross-case thematic analysis:** extracts evidence across interviews to build a wider explanation.

Hybrid forms are legitimate, but disclose how the move from whole interview to theme changes context and meaning.

## Interpretive cycle

1. Listen to complete recordings and read field memos.
2. Build individual chronologies and life-context summaries.
3. Code events, practices, relations, meanings, narrative forms, silences, and contradictions.
4. Compare within and across cases.
5. Test claims against documentary and material sources.
6. Seek negative cases and rival explanations.
7. Return to whole lives to check thematic fragments.
8. Revisit narrators when clarification or collaboration is appropriate.
9. Write claims with explicit evidential reach and uncertainty.

## Media shape meaning

Print privileges text; radio foregrounds voice; film adds powerful visual cues; exhibitions and performance reorganize evidence spatially and socially. Choose media for audience and claim, then record how editing, sequence, narration, image, and sound affect interpretation.

For every excerpt retain narrator identifier, recording identifier, timestamp, question context, editing status, access permission, and analytical purpose. Let contrary voices remain visible.

A strong account shows how critically evaluated remembered experience changes the available evidence, scale of history, and authority to interpret it; it does not claim testimony transparently recovers the past.
