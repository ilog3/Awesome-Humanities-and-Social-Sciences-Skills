# History, Community, and Oral Evidence

## What oral history changes

Historical practice serves present social purposes. Choosing whose testimony enters the record changes the social composition of history itself. Oral history can recover workers, women, families, migrants, minority communities, and ordinary local actors whose experience is thinly represented in official archives.

Its democratic promise is not automatic. The researcher still chooses the topic, narrators, questions, extracts, archive rules, and public form. Make those choices visible.

## A source among sources

Oral transmission preceded modern document-centered history. Recorded interviewing adds a preservable trace of voice, timing, interaction, and expression. Compare sources by their production conditions, not by a simple oral/written hierarchy. Official records may omit, classify, euphemize, or speak from administrative interests; testimony may compress chronology or reflect later identities. Each can expose the other's blind spots.

## Community orientation

A community project can reconnect generations, stimulate local archives, support museums or teaching, and allow participants to shape historical priorities. Build participation into question selection, collecting, interpretation, and return of results when the purpose calls for it.

## Diagnostic prompts

- Which voices dominate the existing record?
- Which absences result from institutional power, literacy, survival, or previous historians' choices?
- Who should control access and representation?
- What will participants receive besides a request for memories?
- Could the project romanticize tradition, mute conflict, or reproduce local elites?
