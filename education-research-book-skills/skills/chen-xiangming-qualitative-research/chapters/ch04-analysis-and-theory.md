# Analysis, Theory, and Writing

## Concurrent analysis

Begin during collection. After each encounter:

1. secure and label materials;
2. expand fieldnotes promptly;
3. write a case/encounter summary;
4. distinguish description, participant meaning, inference, and question;
5. memo emerging concepts, contradictions, and method effects;
6. decide what to sample, ask, observe, or verify next.

Maintain a case file, source index, coding record, methodological log, reflexive journal, and evolving conceptual map.

## Coding and local concepts

Read repeatedly before fragmenting. Mark meaningful units and use participants' own terms when they organize local experience. Define each code, include and exclude examples, compare across cases and contexts, revise overlaps, and preserve links to source locations.

Coding is a tool for thinking, not the analysis itself.

## Categorical and contextual analysis

- **Categorical analysis** gathers material by concepts or themes and enables comparison.
- **Contextual analysis** preserves event, case, temporal, relational, and narrative wholeness.

Move between them through a hermeneutic circle: interpret parts in relation to wholes and revise the whole through the parts. Use matrices, timelines, event structures, case narratives, diagrams, and negative cases to prevent decontextualization.

## Theory construction

Build concepts and relations proportionate to the evidence. A grounded-theory route may use:

1. open coding to surface concepts;
2. axial/relational coding to examine conditions, interactions, strategies, and consequences;
3. selective/core coding to integrate a central explanatory structure;
4. constant comparison, theoretical sampling, memos, process analysis, and a conditional matrix.

Do not mechanically force all projects through three coding stages or claim theory when the result is only a theme list. Show the data-to-concept-to-relation chain, alternatives, exceptions, and scope.

## Writing as analysis and representation

Writing constructs an account and exercises power. Use thick description, contextualized evidence, interpretive commentary, reflexive disclosure, and a visible warrant for each claim. Choose a form suited to audience and ethics; distinguish participant voice from authorial paraphrase. Composite cases and altered details require disclosure and must not distort the analysis.

Protect against deductive identification, sensationalism, coerced co-authorship, and quotation without context.
