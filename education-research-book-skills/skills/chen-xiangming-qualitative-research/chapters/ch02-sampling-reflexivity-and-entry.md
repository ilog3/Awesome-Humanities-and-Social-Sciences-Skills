# Sampling, Reflexivity, and Field Entry

## Purposive sampling

Select information-rich cases for a named analytic reason. Common strategies include:

- extreme or deviant cases;
- intensity cases;
- maximum variation;
- homogeneous groups;
- typical cases;
- stratified purposive cases;
- critical cases;
- theory-driven or confirming/disconfirming cases;
- snowball or chain referral;
- opportunistic/emergent cases;
- criterion cases;
- politically important cases;
- combined strategies;
- convenience cases, disclosed as a constraint.

Sampling decisions depend on the question, access, time, relationship, case characteristics, evolving analysis, and ethics. State inclusion and exclusion, who identifies cases, what information each case can add, stopping logic, and blind spots. Do not promise statistical generalization.

## Researcher as situated instrument

Examine gender, age, language, cultural background, education, institutional role, status, appearance, personality, biography, theoretical preference, and emotional response when relevant. Ask what each makes easier to see, harder to hear, or safer to say.

Maintain a reflexive log with:

- current assumption;
- triggering event;
- emotional and bodily response;
- possible effect on relation, attention, or interpretation;
- evidence for and against the inference;
- methodological or ethical response.

## Insider and outsider

Treat insider/outsider position as multidimensional and changing. Insider knowledge can increase access and tacit understanding while normalizing assumptions and intensifying role conflict. Outsider distance can make the familiar visible while increasing misinterpretation and mistrust. Maintain a productive double awareness rather than claiming complete membership or detachment.

## Field entry

1. Map formal and informal gatekeepers.
2. Prepare a truthful, audience-appropriate account of the study.
3. Negotiate access without trading away participant rights or analytic independence.
4. Obtain continuing participant consent separately.
5. Clarify roles, time, recording, privacy, data use, withdrawal, feedback, and reciprocity.
6. Enter gradually, observe the effects of presence, and revisit agreements.
7. Treat refusal and failed entry as information and an ethical boundary—not simply an obstacle to overcome.

Power is present in access, questioning, interpretation, authorship, and exit. Record who benefits, who bears risk, and who controls representation.
