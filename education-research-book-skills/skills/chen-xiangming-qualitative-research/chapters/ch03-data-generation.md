# Data Generation

## Interviewing as a speech event

An interview is a purposeful, relational conversation shaped by language, setting, roles, sequence, audience, power, and the researcher's responses. It produces an account in interaction; it does not simply extract a stored fact.

### Prepare

- choose time and place for safety, privacy, comfort, and relevance;
- negotiate purpose, recording, confidentiality, use, and withdrawal;
- build a flexible guide from the research question;
- begin with accessible experience before sensitive or abstract questions;
- plan closing, debrief, and follow-up.

### Ask, listen, respond

Prefer open, concrete, clear questions: “Can you take me through the last time…?” Follow with probes for example, sequence, meaning, comparison, exception, actor, consequence, and the participant's own words. Avoid stacked, leading, accusatory, presupposing, or prematurely theoretical questions.

Listen behaviorally, cognitively, and emotionally. Permit silence. Use recognition, repetition, rephrasing, summary, clarification, and carefully judged self-disclosure. Avoid argumentative or evaluative responses that recruit compliance.

Record verbal content, relevant nonverbal and contextual information, interactional turns, and immediate reflection. Do not infer inner states from nonverbal behavior without corroboration.

## Focus groups

Use focus groups when interaction and collective construction matter, not as a cheap bundle of individual interviews. Define composition and homogeneity/variation, moderator role, space, sequence, confidentiality limits, participation balance, dominant voices, silences, alliances, disagreement, and group-produced meanings. Never promise that participants will keep one another's disclosures confidential.

## Observation

Move iteratively:

1. open observation to understand the field;
2. progressive focusing based on questions and emerging analysis;
3. responsive interaction that follows meaningful events while minimizing unnecessary disruption.

Record time, place, actors, activities, sequence, talk, objects, space, and sensory context. Separate:

- observed event;
- near-verbatim speech;
- researcher's inference;
- emotion or bodily response;
- methodological decision;
- theoretical memo.

Participation changes what can be seen and what happens. Document the role and its effects.

## Artifacts

Collect official documents, personal materials, images, tools, records, digital objects, spaces, and other traces according to provenance and consent. Ask who produced the artifact, for whom, under what conditions, what it was meant to do, how it circulated, what is absent, and how current actors interpret it.

Cross-source comparison should expose difference and reactivity, not manufacture artificial agreement.
