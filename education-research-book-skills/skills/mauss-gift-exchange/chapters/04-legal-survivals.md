# 古代法律与经济中的遗存

Mauss compares Indo-European and Semitic legal traditions to show that the clean modern separation among person, thing, sale, gift, pledge, and contract is historically produced.

## Roman law

Early forms connect transferred things with persons, family power, ritual form, pledge, and sanction. Concepts later treated as distinct personal and real rights retain traces of an earlier attachment between giver and thing. Use the comparison to question when ownership becomes fully alienable and when formality makes obligation effective.

## Classical Indian law

Religious and legal texts treat the gift as morally potent. Gifts, food, alms, priestly recipients, merit, impurity, and repayment form a system in which the thing can affect giver and receiver. What may be given, accepted, or retained depends on status and ritual consequence.

## Germanic law

Gifts, pledges, hospitality, wagers, and reciprocal valuables help create peace and guarantee commitments. The pledge is not merely collateral; it may retain part of the person and compel performance. Terms connecting gift and poison illustrate the ambivalence of what is received: benefit, danger, and obligation.

## How to use the comparisons

Do not claim a single evolutionary sequence from gift to market. Instead compare bundles:

`person–thing attachment | ritual form | witnesses | enforceability | return | status | alienability`

Ask which elements persist in modern inheritance, donations, sponsorship, intellectual credit, ceremonial awards, and relational contracting.
