# 太平洋交换体系

## Polynesian total prestations

Samoan exchanges accompany marriage, birth, initiation, illness, funerals, and trade. Categories such as *oloa* and *tonga* connect property to gendered kinship, land, lineage, fosterage, prestige, and continuing flows between families. The exchanged person or treasure can act as a channel of relations rather than an alienable unit.

Māori *taonga*, *mana*, *utu*, and *hau* show a world in which property, person, group, territory, spirituality, justice, and return are entangled. Giving, receiving, and reciprocating support alliance; withholding may carry social or sacred sanctions.

## Gifts to humans, spirits, and nature

Exchange may include ancestors, gods, animals, and natural forces. Sacrifice, alms, festival, and destruction can be ways of making recipients or powers reciprocate. Do not separate “religious” action from subsistence and politics too quickly.

## Melanesian extension

In New Caledonia and other settings, ceremonial words, feasts, valuables, and ancestors bind parties across time. Gifts stitch groups together while preserving distinctions.

The Trobriand **kula** circulates named valuables in opposite directions through long-distance partnerships. It coexists with barter, provisioning, chiefs' redistribution, ritual, magic, sexuality, rivalry, and ordinary exchange. A kula valuable matters through its itinerary, former holders, prestige, and obligation—not utilitarian consumption.

## Comparative questions

- What categories distinguish durable treasure, consumable goods, tools, and imported objects?
- Who may possess, display, lend, pass, or destroy each item?
- Does circulation create peace, mobility, rank, kinship, or access to resources?
- What must be returned, to whom, when, and with what increase?
