# Rackets, Politics, and Obligation

## Core idea

Informal groups are connected to gambling, policing, clubs, employment, and ward politics by brokers and reciprocal obligations. Formal legality alone does not describe how authority operates.

## Frameworks introduced

- **Brokerage chain:** Trace people who connect otherwise separate groups or institutions.
- **Political obligation:** Examine favors, access, protection, turnout, jobs, and loyalty as reciprocal relations.
- **Formal–informal coupling:** Compare official organizational charts with actual channels for decisions and resources.
- **Organizational change through faction:** Follow coalitions across a club's founding, conflict, reorganization, and leadership turnover.

## Worked example

For a school-community initiative, map the principal, parent association, neighborhood leaders, local officials, and informal organizers. Follow one concrete resource request from initiation to completion. The formal committee may approve it, while an informal broker secures attendance, resolves opposition, or obtains municipal cooperation. Report both chains and their costs.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating illegal activity as external to normal social organization.
- Reducing political support to ideology while ignoring accumulated obligations.
- Calling all exchange corruption without distinguishing coercion, reciprocity, brokerage, and public service.

## Key takeaways

1. Follow transactions and obligations across institutional boundaries.
2. Study political organizations through careers and events, not formal rules alone.
3. Analyze who benefits, who bears risk, and who cannot access brokers.
