# Corner Gangs, Status, and Mobility

## Core idea

Individual performance and ambition are organized by group position, loyalty, and institutional ties. The Norton gang and the college-oriented club reveal different relations between peer organization and mobility.

## Frameworks introduced

- **Interaction–status pattern:** Repeated attention, initiative, following, and successful coordination make rank visible.
- **Leadership as enacted coordination:** A leader is the person whose backing turns suggestions into collective action and who maintains ties across members.
- **Performance and standing:** Compare performance in activities such as bowling with existing rank; changes may reveal strain or reorganization.
- **Loyalty–mobility tension:** Movement toward outside institutions can weaken internal trust or require brokerage between worlds.

## Worked example

To test leadership in a student peer group, do not ask only “Who is the leader?” Observe three decisions. For each, note who proposes, who gains the floor, whose approval recruits others, who handles dissent, and who carries out the decision. Compare this interaction map with self-reports and formal titles. A member who talks most may not coordinate action.

## Anti-patterns

- Inferring rank from charisma or one interview.
- Treating mobility as individual motivation detached from group loyalties.
- Freezing “corner” and “college” into psychological types.

## Key takeaways

1. Rank is a repeated relational accomplishment.
2. Group change becomes clearest during campaigns, competition, and splits.
3. Mobility changes both the mover and the network around them.
4. Compare groups rather than moralizing their differences.
