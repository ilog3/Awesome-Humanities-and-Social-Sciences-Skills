# Fieldnotes, Analysis, and Redesign

## Core idea

Long immersion yields knowledge only when observations become inspectable records and emerging patterns redirect fieldwork.

## Frameworks introduced

- **Event-sequence note:** Record setting, participants, chronological interaction, quoted language, audience reactions, outcome, and later interpretation.
- **Relational diagram:** Visualize membership, rank, ties, factions, and brokers; update it after consequential events.
- **Focused comparison:** Once a pattern appears, seek parallel and contrary events across groups.
- **Iterative redesign:** Replace speculative initial plans with questions generated from sustained observation.

## Worked example

If participants identify two different leaders, observe a series of decisions rather than choosing one testimony. For each event, track whose suggestion receives support, whose dissent stops action, who carries messages, and who repairs conflict. Compare the event matrix with both informants' accounts. The disagreement may reflect distinct settings or kinds of leadership.

## Practical note cycle

1. Write brief unobtrusive jottings only when safe.
2. Expand them promptly into chronological fieldnotes.
3. Separate observed detail, participant explanation, and researcher inference.
4. Add an analytic memo with a proposed relation.
5. List the next observation that could disconfirm it.
6. Update the network or event diagram.

## Anti-patterns

- Accumulating diaries without conceptual comparison.
- Retrospectively cleaning uncertainty out of notes.
- Turning participant categories into researcher conclusions without testing.
- Keeping the original research plan after the field reveals a different structure.

## Key takeaways

1. Interaction sequences are stronger evidence than generalized impressions.
2. Simple counts and diagrams can test qualitative patterns without replacing context.
3. Analysis should continuously redirect observation.
