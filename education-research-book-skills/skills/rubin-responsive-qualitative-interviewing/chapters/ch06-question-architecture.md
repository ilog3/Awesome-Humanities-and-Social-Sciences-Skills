# Chapters 7–9: 主要问题、追踪问题与探测性问题

## Core idea

Build the interview from three complementary question types. Main questions establish domains, probes improve the present answer, and follow-up questions pursue discoveries across the study.

## Good answers

Seek:

- **depth:** underlying meaning and process;
- **detail:** names, steps, roles, time, context;
- **vividness:** concrete episodes and language;
- **nuance:** qualifications, exceptions, ambiguity, and competing views;
- **richness:** connections among concepts and experiences.

## Main questions

Keep them understandable, open enough for partners to answer in their own terms, and specific enough to address the study. Use broad scene questions, focused event questions, examples, comparison, and process reconstruction. Ask about the wider context when it changes meaning.

## Probes

Use short prompts to clarify meaning, regulate pace, obtain sequence or example, recover omitted actors, identify evidence, and check understanding. Examples: “What happened next?”, “What does that term mean here?”, “Can you give a specific occasion?”, “How did you know?”, “Who saw it differently?”

## Follow-up questions

Prepare them during and after analysis when a partner introduces a new concept, oversimplifies, omits information, tells an unfinished story, contradicts another account, or suggests a mechanism. Follow the idea across later partners and return to earlier partners when appropriate.

## Three interview structures

- **Floodgate:** one broad opening allows a long narrative; useful when the partner has a coherent experience and interruption would damage it.
- **Tree-and-branch:** main topics are branches systematically covered with probes; useful for comparable coverage.
- **River-and-channel:** follow the partner's flow while steering periodically into required channels; useful for responsive depth with bounded focus.

## Worked example

Main: “Walk me through how a student support decision is made.” Probe: “Who first notices the problem?” Follow-up from early analysis: “Several people used the phrase ‘real need.’ What makes a need real here, and who can contest that judgment?”

## Anti-patterns

Leading, double questions, abstract “why” without an event, jargon, rapid topic switching, repeated challenge, and probes that display disbelief.
