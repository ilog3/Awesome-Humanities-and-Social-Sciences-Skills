# Leadership, Politics, and Capacity

## Core idea

Leaders make reform possible by assembling participation, protecting experimentation, aligning structures, and managing conflict. Charisma without distributed capacity leaves change fragile.

## Frameworks introduced

- **Political coalition:** Build enough legitimate support among teachers, students, families, district actors, and external partners to sustain contested change.
- **Structural alignment:** Reallocate time, load, schedule, staffing, space, budget, and assessment around reform purposes.
- **Distributed interpretation:** Give teachers and students real authority to translate and revise principles.
- **Protection plus accountability:** Shield experimentation while requiring evidence of student experience and learning.
- **Succession test:** Ask whether reform survives leader turnover because knowledge, authority, and routines are distributed.

## Worked example

A principal champions personalization but gives teachers no shared planning time and controls all interpretation. Early adopters create a successful enclave; expansion stalls and turnover threatens the project. The next step is not motivational messaging. It is to redistribute decision rights, redesign schedules, fund collaboration, and include student evidence.

## Anti-patterns

- Reform by mandate while claiming teacher ownership.
- Consultation without decision authority.
- Relying on one heroic leader or teacher.
- Protecting innovation from all critical evidence.
- Ignoring district rules and external tests.

## Key takeaways

1. Reform leadership is political and organizational work.
2. Voice matters when it changes decisions.
3. Capacity and succession determine endurance.
