# Longitudinal Ethnographic Method

## Core idea

Five years across eight contrasting schools makes reform visible as a changing process rather than a one-time outcome. Multi-scale evidence connects movement, school, classroom, teacher, and student.

## Design elements

- purposeful variation among school types, sizes, populations, and reform forms;
- repeated visits and interviews with supporters and critics;
- classroom observations and student work;
- student surveys used alongside ethnography;
- school documents, press, yearbooks, teacher memos, and network records;
- personnel, enrollment, policy, and resource histories;
- within-case chronology before cross-case comparison;
- pseudonyms and formal research agreements.

## Worked example

To study a current learning-community reform, sample schools that vary in entry motive, governance, scale, and student population. Build annual case histories; follow one classroom and one teacher over time; interview dissenters; collect schedules, tasks, assessments, and student work; then compare depth, spread, ownership, and endurance.

## Reflexive questions

- Did access through reform leaders overrepresent enthusiasts?
- Did the study's presence or reports change local politics?
- What disappeared between visits?
- Whose interpretation of “reform” organized data collection?
- How do surveys and ethnographic accounts contradict or qualify one another?

## Key takeaways

1. Longitudinal design is essential for reform trajectories.
2. Compare scales without assuming policy flows downward unchanged.
3. Include critics and student experience deliberately.
4. Treat pseudonymity as risk reduction, not complete anonymity.
