# Reform and Resistance in Schools and Classrooms

Use longitudinal ethnography to explain how reform travels from a movement's principles into schools, classrooms, roles, schedules, assessment, relationships, and politics—and why implementation becomes uneven.

## Central proposition

**School reform is both an educational and a political endeavor.** A compelling pedagogy is insufficient when time, authority, participation, resources, accountability, leadership, and institutional interests remain unaddressed.

## Reform object: CES common principles

Treat the Coalition of Essential Schools principles as a historically situated reform grammar:

- intellectual focus and **less is more**;
- common goals with personalized means;
- personalization and manageable teacher responsibility;
- **student-as-worker**, teacher as coach;
- demonstration/exhibition of mastery rather than seat-time accumulation;
- a tone of trust, decency, fairness, and high expectations;
- teachers and principals as generalists before narrow specialists;
- schedules, staffing, planning time, and budgets aligned with the principles.

Do not score adoption by slogans. Trace how each principle is translated into concrete routines and experiences.

## Workflow

1. **Reconstruct the reform theory.** Specify the principles, proposed learning mechanism, intended actors, and required organizational conditions. Read [chapters/ch01-ces-principles-and-reform-theory.md](chapters/ch01-ces-principles-and-reform-theory.md).
2. **Map local interpretations and agendas.** Ask what leaders, teachers, students, families, district actors, and reform staff think the reform is for. Read [chapters/ch02-local-agendas-and-school-trajectories.md](chapters/ch02-local-agendas-and-school-trajectories.md).
3. **Build a longitudinal trajectory.** Identify entry, early coalition, resource decisions, personnel changes, crises, expansion, retrenchment, routinization, and outcomes.
4. **Compare whole-school cases.** Use common dimensions without erasing local history. Read [chapters/ch03-cross-school-comparison.md](chapters/ch03-cross-school-comparison.md).
5. **Follow reform into classrooms.** Observe tasks, teacher–student roles, curriculum coordination, time, assessment, student meaning, and work products. Read [chapters/ch04-teacher-classroom-and-student-response.md](chapters/ch04-teacher-classroom-and-student-response.md).
6. **Interpret resistance diagnostically.** Distinguish principled dissent, workload defense, incoherence detection, identity threat, implementation overload, material constraint, political exclusion, and routine inertia.
7. **Analyze leadership as political work.** Examine coalition building, protection, distributed authority, time and resource alignment, continuity, and participation. Read [chapters/ch05-leadership-politics-and-capacity.md](chapters/ch05-leadership-politics-and-capacity.md).
8. **Assess depth, spread, ownership, and endurance separately.** A showcase classroom, enthusiastic principal, or network membership does not demonstrate school-wide institutionalization.
9. **Design evidence across scales.** Combine field observation, interviews with supporters and critics, student evidence, surveys, documents, and reform history. Read [chapters/ch06-longitudinal-ethnographic-method.md](chapters/ch06-longitudinal-ethnographic-method.md).
10. **Produce an actionable diagnosis.** Identify the smallest set of educational, political, and structural changes needed for the next phase—not a generic call for commitment.

## Required outputs

Provide reform theory, actor/agenda map, school trajectory, classroom enactment evidence, student experience, resistance typology, leadership and capacity analysis, cross-case comparison, implementation depth/spread/ownership/endurance, rival explanations, and next-step design.

Use [patterns.md](patterns.md), [glossary.md](glossary.md), and [cheatsheet.md](cheatsheet.md).

## Guardrails

- Do not label disagreement as resistance until the contested object and reason are known.
- Do not infer classroom change from policy documents, network affiliation, or professional-development attendance.
- Do not treat teachers as implementers without discretion or students as passive recipients.
- Do not measure reform only by standardized outcomes when its theory concerns relationships, intellectual work, autonomy, or exhibitions.
- Do not celebrate personalization without checking selection, exclusion, workload, and scale.
- Do not generalize eight U.S. secondary-school cases from the late 1980s/early 1990s directly to another system.
