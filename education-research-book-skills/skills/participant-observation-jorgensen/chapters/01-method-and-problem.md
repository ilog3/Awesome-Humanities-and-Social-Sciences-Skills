# 方法论与研究问题

## Seven interlocking characteristics

Participant observation:

1. is suited to a wide range of questions about human life;
2. seeks insider understandings of everyday interaction and meaning;
3. develops practical, interpretive theoretical principles;
4. uses an open, flexible process that changes with field experience;
5. usually takes the form of deep case study;
6. uses participation strategically to approach otherwise inaccessible experience;
7. centers direct observation and experience while allowing interviews, documents, and other methods.

## Suitability test

Use it when the phenomenon is obscured from public view, differs between insider and outsider accounts, is insufficiently understood, or involves processes, interactions, and meanings that cannot be captured well by standardized instruments alone.

Avoid claiming statistical representativeness. A deep case may generate, refine, challenge, or illustrate theory, but its transfer depends on mechanism, comparison, and contextual similarity.

## Defining the problem

Begin either with a broad question and find a setting, or with a setting and discover a question. Move repeatedly between the two. Phrase questions interrogatively and refine them as evidence accumulates.

Concepts should arise from how people use meanings in practice. Operationalize them with several observable indicators instead of one nominal definition. Track how the researcher's biography, theory, social problem orientation, sponsor, and institutional setting shape the problem.

## Validity, reliability, and ethics

Aim for accurate, trustworthy accounts through prolonged exposure, multiple evidence sources, practical testing of concepts, explicit reasoning, negative cases, peer challenge, and—where appropriate—independent comparison. Do not pretend to be value-free. Make interests and procedures examinable, respect participants' rights, and assess consequences for their lives.
