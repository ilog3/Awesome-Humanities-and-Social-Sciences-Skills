# Foundations and Design

## Methodological fit

Use grounded theory when the aim is to explain how people act, interact, adapt, manage a problem, or move through a process under varying conditions. Description may be a legitimate endpoint, but calling the result grounded theory requires conceptual integration beyond description.

The approach draws on pragmatism and symbolic interactionism: meanings arise in action and interaction, realities are interpreted, and useful concepts should explain variation in experience.

## Starting question

Begin broad enough to allow discovery but bounded enough for access and ethics. Prefer a process-oriented question such as “How do novice teachers learn to handle public error in high-stakes classrooms?” over a predetermined-variable question.

## Data and literature

Data may include interviews, observations, documents, memoirs, images, media, existing datasets, and researcher experience. Evaluate provenance and relevance. Literature may sensitize the researcher, suggest comparisons, expose gaps, and later position the theory; it must not become a template that preclassifies all data.

## Analytic preparation

Maintain source identifiers, versioned transcripts or fieldnotes, reflexive notes, and a decision log. Separate description of events from analytic memos while linking them. Begin analysis early enough that it can guide further collection.
