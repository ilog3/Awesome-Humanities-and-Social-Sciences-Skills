# Memos and Diagrams

## Memoing is analysis

Write memos from the first analytic encounter. Their value is not formal elegance but making thought cumulative and inspectable. Move from exploratory notes to category memos, comparative memos, methodological memos, reflexive memos, and integrative memos as analysis develops.

Every memo should include a date, title, author, source links, focal concept, evidence, comparison, provisional interpretation, uncertainty, and next analytic or sampling action.

## Strong memo structure

1. Define the concept in active terms.
2. Cite contrasting incidents.
3. Identify properties and dimensions.
4. Propose conditions, actions/interactions, and consequences.
5. Compare with neighboring concepts.
6. Record negative evidence.
7. State questions and theoretical-sampling needs.

Update rather than silently overwrite; the changing memo is part of the audit trail.

## Diagrams

Use diagrams to test relations, sequence, hierarchy, feedback, and missing links. Early diagrams may be rough. Later diagrams should show how categories connect around an explanatory storyline. If the same memo seems to describe two codes, test whether they are one category, related categories, or an unexamined condition–action relation.

Fieldnotes record what occurred; memos analyze what it may mean. Keep both and link them.
